What the racers want

The same system drawn as motivation rather than architecture. Not what the code does — what each creature is paid for, what it is punished for, and how those incentives add up to the gait you actually see on screen.

Five diagrams. Every number is measured: reward weights from chase_env_cfg.py and Reward_WormLoco.cs, earnings from the final iteration of the 2,016-iteration boy_chase_flat run, speeds from each port's eval manifest. Skinned to match the DOCS suite; coral is deliberately absent, because red and green are reserved in this project as the racer-controller legend.

A trained racer has no plan. It has no map of the track, no memory of the last race, and in one case no idea where the finish line is. What it has is a number that went up or down a few million times during training, and a set of weights shaped by that number. Everything a racer appears to want is a fossil of that number.

So the honest way to describe these creatures is not by their architecture. It is by their incentives.

1 · The loop that made them

Work advances clockwise. Every pass writes back into one shared thing — the weights.

Only one station in that ring is a design decision. The reward function is the entire personality of the creature; the rest is arithmetic.

2 · Five wants, stacked by how often they pay

The same creature is chasing all five at once. They differ enormously in how often they fire — and that, not their headline weight, is what actually shapes behaviour.

That stack is also the clearest way to see why mean reward is the wrong promotion metric. L4 is potential-based: its sum over an episode is exactly the metres of ground the creature closed. L2 and L3 accumulate per step. So a creature that survives twice as long collects twice as much shaping — and outscores a faster creature that finished early. The number goes up. The racing gets worse.

3 · How it ends, and what each ending is worth

Four exits, checked in this order, every single step. Three of them cost the creature something.

4 · What it actually earns, once trained

IsaacBox's reward budget at the final iteration, seventeen terms grouped into eight. Positive is earned; negative is what earning it cost.

Two rows in that chart say something a weights table cannot. targets_reached carries the largest positive weight in the entire config — 5.0 — and earns 0.012 per second, because it fires perhaps twice in a twenty-second episode. And termination_penalty carries the largest weight of all, −200, while costing 0.005 per second. A huge penalty that is almost never collected is not a wasted term: it is what a working balance term looks like from the outside.

A reward weight tells you what the designer intended. What the term earns tells you what the creature learned.

5 · Fast, or aimed — almost none of them are both

Every racer placed by measured speed against whether anything in its observation vector tells it where to go.

This is the single most useful thing the five diagrams say together. A creature cannot want something it cannot perceive. Fido's problem is not motivational and not an integration bug — it reproduces in the original drop's own verification scene at its own settings. It is an input problem, and the only fix is retraining with a heading term.

WHAT THE DIAGRAMS AGREE ON

Motivation is authored once

  • Of the six stations in the loop, only the reward is a design decision. The rest is arithmetic.
  • Shaping terms never run at race time, so they can be retuned freely — until the next training run.
  • The one term whose episode sum is a physical quantity is progress. It is also the only one that needs no timestep scaling.

WHERE IT GOES WRONG

Density beats weight

  • A −200 weight collected twice an hour matters less than a −0.005 weight collected 200 times a second.
  • The four-day step-scaling bug multiplied the dense terms by four and left the sparse ones alone — silently changing what the creature wanted.
  • Mean reward rises with survival time. Promote on ELO from actual races.

THE HARD LIMIT

Perception bounds desire

  • Fido: 33 floats, none of them a goal. Fastest gait in the project, cannot aim it.
  • IsaacH1 gets a velocity command, not a target — it walks where told, not where it needs to be.
  • IsaacBox has target_pos_b in slots 9–12. That is the whole difference.
